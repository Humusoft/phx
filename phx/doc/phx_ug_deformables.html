Soft Bodies, Flexible Bodies and Particles

Objects that deform, and granular material in bulk

Overview

Besides rigid bodies, a scene can hold four kinds of objects made of many points. All four are top-level objects like a phx.Body: create them in the axes, or hand them to the simulation, and they collide with the rigid bodies of the scene.

ObjectMade ofTypical use
phx.SoftBody A triangle mesh whose vertices are held together by its edges, optionally inflated by a gas pressure. Cloth, cushions, balls, airbags.
phx.FlexibleBody A mesh of tetrahedra of an elastic material given by its Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio; reports the von Mises stress. Rubber blocks, pads and bushings, beams, springy tines.
phx.Particles Many small spheres of one radius, read and drawn all at once. Sand, gravel, grain, beads.
phx.Liquid Particles kept apart by the pressure of the liquid (smoothed particle hydrodynamics), drawn as a smooth surface. Water in tanks, sloshing, dam breaks, floating bodies.

Soft and flexible bodies collide with rigid bodies and particles, not with each other. Their mesh, Vertices in the body frame, is the rest shape: it is placed at Position and Orientation and read, together with the material and the initial Velocities of the vertices (in the body frame as well), once, when the simulation creates the object; every new simulation starts again from it. The other settings, such as LinearDamping or Friction, apply at any time. The present state is in CurrentVertices and CurrentVelocities, in the global frame: the deformed mesh while the simulation runs, the last one after it.

Particles and liquids have no rest shape: their Vertices and Velocities (in the frame of the group) are the state itself, and a new simulation goes on from where the last one ended. They have CurrentVertices and CurrentVelocities too, so that code reading the present points of any of these objects uses one name.

Soft bodies

A phx.SoftBody spreads its Mass evenly over the vertices of the mesh. Its edges keep their length up to their Compliance (the inverse of stiffness; 0 makes them rigid), and BendCompliance adds edges across adjacent triangles that resist bending. A closed mesh can hold a gas Pressure that pushes on its faces, which turns it into a ball or an airbag. The mesh comes from a shape ("Shape", see Meshes from shapes) or as Vertices and Faces of your own; list the faces of a closed mesh counter-clockwise seen from outside, as convhull gives them.

clf; view(3); axis equal; grid on; camlight headlight;

floor = phx.Body("Type", "static", "Position", [0 0 -0.1], "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [2 2 0.2]});
ball = phx.SoftBody(gca, "Shape", {"Sphere", "Diameter", 0.5, "ElementResolution", 16}, "Position", [0 0 1], ...
    "Mass", 0.5, "Compliance", 1e-3, "Pressure", 15, "Iterations", 10);
sim = phx.Simulation(gca);
sim.step(4, 800);                    % the ball bounces, squashes and settles

The vertices collide as spheres of VertexRadius. Iterations sets the solver substeps of the soft body per simulation step: more of them bring stiff edges closer to their compliance.

Flexible bodies

A phx.FlexibleBody is an elastic solid. Give it a mesh of tetrahedra and a material (Density, YoungsModulus, PoissonsRatio). The mesh can come from a shape ("Shape", see Meshes from shapes), from PDE Toolbox ("Mesh", PHX only reads it), for example for a part of any form in an STL file, or as Vertices and Tetrahedra of your own. The body deforms as a material that also holds for large rotations, and colors its surface by the von Mises stress when you choose a Colormap.

The elements are quadratic tetrahedra: a K×4 mesh gets a vertex in the middle of each edge. They bend without stiffening on a coarse mesh and are solved by Newton iterations until the force left unbalanced falls below Tolerance, so their stiffness does not depend on Iterations, which only limits the iterations per step.

clf; view(3); axis equal; grid on; camlight headlight;

wall = phx.Body("Type", "static", "Position", [-0.05 0 0], "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [0.1 0.3 0.3]});
beam = phx.FlexibleBody(gca, "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [1.05 0.1 0.1]}, "Position", [0.475 0 0], ...
    "YoungsModulus", 2e7, "LinearDamping", 2, "Colormap", turbo(64), "ColorRange", [0 3e5]);
phx.IntersectionWeld(beam, wall);    % the wall holds the end of the beam inside it
sim = phx.Simulation(gca);
sim.step(3, 600);                    % the beam sags under its weight, stressed most at the wall

The body touches rigid bodies at its vertices only, so keep them well closer together than the radius of a curved body it touches. The tetrahedra suit strains up to some 10–20 %.

Meshes from shapes

The simplest mesh comes from a shape, given to "Shape" as an object or as the same cell array that phx.Body takes. A flexible body fills the shape with tetrahedra; its material is its own Density or Mass (not both), as for a rigid body. A soft body covers the shape with triangles and takes only its geometry: a soft body is a skin, and its Mass stays its own.

How fine the mesh is, the ElementResolution of the shape sets, just as Segments sets how fine the shape is drawn: a number of cells across the shape, independent of its size in metres. A single number counts the cells across the thinnest section and cuts the rest of the shape into cells as close to cubes as possible; a vector gives the number of cells in each direction of the shape and lets the cells be longer in one of them. Each cell is split into six tetrahedra.

ShapeA number counts the cells acrossA vectorDefault
Boxthe smallest side[nx ny nz]2
Spherethe diameter—5
Cylinderthe smaller of the diameter and the height[diameter height]2
Conethe smaller of the larger diameter and the height[diameter height]2
Capsulethe diameter[diameter Height]2
Tubethe smaller of the wall and the height[across around along][1 24 8]

The defaults are chosen for flexible bodies, which cost far more per step than soft ones; they bend a beam within a few percent of beam theory. A soft body usually wants a finer surface, which is cheap: a coarse one is easy to see, so raise ElementResolution until the soft body looks smooth. Mind that a single number on a thin plate or a thin wall makes many small cubes; there a vector with longer cells costs far less, as the default of a tube does.

The tetrahedra of the round shapes are curved: the vertices in the middles of their edges on the surface lie on the true surface, so even a coarse mesh has nearly the volume, and so the mass, of the shape. The surfaces for soft bodies space their vertices about evenly, so that a soft body spreads its mass evenly: a sphere is covered by a subdivided icosahedron, the other round shapes by rings of vertices.

clf; view(3); axis equal; grid on; camlight headlight;

floor = phx.Body("Type", "static", "Position", [0 0 -0.1], "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [2 2 0.2]});
bumper = phx.FlexibleBody(gca, "Shape", {"Cylinder", "Diameter", 0.3, "Height", 0.4}, "Density", 1100, ...
    "Position", [-0.3 0 0.6], "YoungsModulus", 1e6);
pillow = phx.SoftBody(gca, "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [0.4 0.4 0.1], "ElementResolution", 2}, ...
    "Position", [0.3 0 0.6], "Mass", 0.3, "Compliance", 1e-4, "Pressure", 50);
sim = phx.Simulation(gca);
sim.step(2, 400);

The shapes generate the meshes with generateVolumetricMesh and generateSurfaceMesh, which you can also call yourself, for example to scale a sphere into an ellipsoid before handing its vertices to a soft body. Revolution, Extrusion and Mesh generate their meshes in a future release; until then, mesh such a part with PDE Toolbox and give it as "Mesh":

gm = generateMesh(fegeometry("part.stl"), Hmax=0.01);    % STL in metres, or scale it first
part = phx.FlexibleBody(gca, "Mesh", gm, "YoungsModulus", 1e6);

Holding and loading a deformable body

No joint, spring or force acts on a soft or flexible body directly. Instead, place a rigid body so that it overlaps the deformable one and join them with phx.IntersectionWeld: every vertex that lies inside the body's shape when the simulation starts is welded to it, like a metal insert held in rubber. Joints, motors, springs and forces then act on the rigid body, and through it on the deformable one, which acts back. A static body clamps the deformable one, as the wall does in the example above.

  • The rigid body should overlap at least three vertices that do not lie on one line, or it cannot pass on a turn; VertexCount tells how many it holds once the simulation runs.
  • It should not be much lighter than the vertices it holds.
  • One rigid body welded into several deformable ones joins them.

Particles

phx.Particles is a group of spheres of one Radius and Density that collide with all bodies and with each other. The group reads their state as N×3 arrays (Vertices and Velocities, in the frame of the group that Position and Orientation place) and draws them as one graphics object, so many thousands of them stay fast; a group holds up to 10 000 particles. A "Shape" fills with particles touching each other, in the frame of the group. Particles can be added (add, in the global frame) and removed (remove) at any time, also while the simulation runs, and pushed by forces of your own (applyForce).

clf; view(3); axis equal; grid on; camlight headlight;

phx.assembly.arena(gca, "Size", [1 1 0.3]);
sand = phx.Particles(gca, "Radius", 0.02, "Density", 1600, "Color", [0.8 0.7 0.5], "RandomTint", 0.3);
sim = phx.Simulation(gca, "EngineSettings", phx.engine.Settings("LengthScale", 0.1));
[x, y] = meshgrid(-0.1:0.05:0.1);
spout = [x(:) y(:) repmat(0.6, numel(x), 1)];
for k = 1:40                         % pour 25 grains every 50 ms
    sand.add(spout, repmat([0 0 -1], height(spout), 1));
    sim.step(0.05, 10);
end
sim.step(1, 200);                    % let the heap settle

By default the particles only slide; "Rotation", true lets them roll as well, at some cost. Sleeping makes a heap at rest cost next to nothing until something touches it. For particles of a few centimetres or less, match the engine tolerances to their size with the LengthScale of phx.engine.Settings, as above; the default suits parts of about a metre and makes small particles slower to simulate.

Liquids

phx.Liquid is a body of liquid made of particles that do not collide with each other: the pressure of the liquid keeps them apart. It flows, splashes and fills containers, and a body in it floats and is carried by the flow, the liquid and the body pushing on each other. A "Shape" fills with particles Spacing apart; Vertices, Velocities, add, remove and applyForce work as for particles. The liquid has a Density, a Viscosity and a SoundSpeed, how stiff it is against compression, and reports the Densities and Pressures of its particles while the simulation runs.

clf; view(3); axis equal; grid on; camlight headlight;

phx.assembly.arena(gca, "Size", [0.8 0.3 0.4], "Thickness", 0.04);
water = phx.Liquid(gca, "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [0.2 0.3 0.3]}, "Position", [-0.3 0 0.15]);
crate = phx.Body(gca, "Position", [-0.3 0 0.33], "Density", 400, ...
    "Shape", {"Box", "Size", [0.08 0.08 0.05], "Color", [0.85 0.65 0.4]});
sim = phx.Simulation(gca, "EngineSettings", phx.engine.Settings("LengthScale", 0.02));
sim.step(1.5, 6000, 40);             % the column collapses and carries the crate along the tank

The liquid is explicit in time, so its step is short: keep the simulation step (the interval over the substeps) under 0.4*Spacing/(SoundSpeed + v), v the fastest flow. The example above steps by 0.25 ms for a Spacing of 2 cm and the default SoundSpeed of 20 m/s. A SoundSpeed of about ten times the fastest flow keeps the liquid nearly incompressible; a higher one only shortens the step. As for particles, set the LengthScale to about the Spacing, or the default contact margin rounds thin walls and lets the liquid into the corners of a tank. A body that only floats on still water of a flat surface is far cheaper with phx.Buoyancy.

Cost. Deformable bodies and particles are solved point by point, so they cost far more per step than rigid bodies of the same size. Start with a coarse mesh or large particles and refine only as far as the result needs it.

Examples

  • phxex_softball — a soft ball dropped on the edge of a rigid cube.
  • phxex_airbag — a sled crash test with a belted dummy thrown into an airbag.
  • phxex_cantilever — a ball dropped on a flexible rubber beam.
  • phxex_soilparticles — an excavator bucket scooping gravel made of particles.
  • phxex_plow — a flexible spring tine plowing granular soil.
  • phxex_dambreak — a water column collapsing in a tank and carrying a floating crate.
  • phxex_balancewater — a half-filled water ball rolled from stop to stop by a tilting platform.
